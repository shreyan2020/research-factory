# Visual design and audience-facing copy

Read this before choosing slide layouts or assets. Apply the audience, purpose, time, and projection conditions from the brief. The aim is a presentation that can be followed while listening, not a document compressed into slides.

## Visible copy versus presenter notes

Do not append generic disclaimers, provenance badges, process narration, or instructions to the presenter on audience-facing slides. Avoid labels such as “Actual participant-preview screenshot”, “Teaching mockup”, “Ask the class”, “Discuss first; then…”, or a visible navigation checklist for a live demo. Put provenance, demo routes, facilitation, timing, and expected answers in notes. A question the audience can answer belongs on the slide.

Preserve a qualification when removing it would materially change the conclusion. Prefer precise wording within the claim or a short local label to a separate caveat paragraph. A constructed scenario must not masquerade as a real incident: establish the scenario as imagined in its setup and retain the construction details in notes. Do not remove required attribution or invent a factual status to avoid a label. The rule is to remove clutter, not to conceal uncertainty.

Example: replace “Teaching mockup — consent screen. Ask the class which boundary failed” with a readable screen and “Would you start this study?” Explain the study and the participant's task before asking. Put the intended answer and screenshot provenance in notes. Do not ask about a technical term that has not been taught.

## Humor, emojis, and illustrations

For Shreyan's interactive teaching, use relevant humor when invited or already present. A dry, uniformly serious deck can miss the brief. Preserve his jokes and improve their setup and payoff instead of replacing them with generic professional prose.

An emoji can be a useful recurring character or cue: for example, a mischievous participant introducing ways a study could be gamed. A short meme-style exchange can expose a failure more clearly than another warning box. Make the joke lead directly to the concept and discussion question. Do not imply all participants behave maliciously. Match the tone to the audience; do not apply classroom humor automatically to a formal research defense.

Do not scatter decorative emojis across every heading. Keep a consistent visual vocabulary, ensure symbols remain understandable with text, and check emoji rendering in the actual export. Removing one disliked illustration is a rejection of that asset, not a permanent ban on images. Do not reintroduce the same rejected asset under another filename.

## Select the visual before the layout

| What the audience needs to understand | Useful starting point | Check |
|---|---|---|
| A real interface or participant experience | Cropped, readable screenshot with a focused annotation | Correct screen, state, and provenance; remove private details |
| A plausible failure scenario | Coherent app mockup or short illustrated sequence | Establish context; depict the requested situation rather than a generic terminal |
| Differences across conditions | Aligned dot/bar plot or small multiples | Common units, denominators, scales, and uncertainty |
| Change over ordered time | Line plot | Actual time spacing, missing observations, and distinct series |
| Spread or heterogeneity | Distribution, box plot, or raw points | Do not replace spread with a mean alone |
| Association | Scatter plot | Label axes; fit a trend only when justified; do not imply causation |
| A causal argument or selection mechanism | Small labeled DAG plus concrete story | Explain nodes, arrows, and conditioning; distinguish assumptions from findings |
| Qualitative evidence | Short contextual quote, evidence comparison, or theme diagram | Preserve attribution and dissent; an attractive theme is not a frequency estimate |
| Sequence, architecture, or study flow | Process diagram or storyboard | Separate roles and stages; arrows have an explicit meaning |
| Exact values | Compact table | Highlight the comparison rather than reading the entire table aloud |

These are choices, not mandatory chart-to-research-type rules. Choose from the actual data and question. Use a part-to-whole graphic only for a valid whole and few readable components; use aligned comparisons when differences are the point.

## Reuse and adapt research figures

Inventory existing paper figures, plots, screenshots, and diagrams before creating replacements. Choose the figure that carries the claim, then simplify its presentation for the room: crop irrelevant panels, enlarge labels, highlight the relevant comparison, or split a dense multipanel figure across a sequence. Preserve units, baselines, uncertainty, and caption meaning. Retain the original and record material adaptation in notes.

Never invent values or redraw a plot approximately from its appearance. Use verified source data for editable charts. If only the original figure is available, retain a legible image and attribution; do not promise data editability. Generated artwork can illustrate a scenario or concept; it must not manufacture an empirical chart, real screenshot, or scientific finding. Use the appropriate image-generation tool when a purposeful illustration helps, then inspect its labels and factual content.

## Compose for projection

Vary layout with the teaching purpose: an enlarged screenshot, a visual comparison, one diagram, a sparse question, and a results chart need different treatments. Do not make every slide the same row of text cards. Whitespace is acceptable; unexplained gaps from hidden objects or bad alignment are not.

Give each slide a clear reading order and focus. Use brief labels, direct chart labeling where possible, consistent units and color meanings, and enough contrast. Do not rely on pale fills or color alone for boundaries: outlines, labels, line styles, or shapes should retain meaning on a washed-out projector. Use a strong outline or clearer fill when the actual display loses card edges. Do not impose a new palette if the user has already selected one.

Preview at presentation size and as a thumbnail. Check axes, legends, citations, crops, and image sharpness. A laptop screenshot cannot prove readability at the back of the classroom. For click builds, check the initial state and every reveal as well as the final slide.
