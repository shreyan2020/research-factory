# Planning, interpretation, and manuscript handoff

## Before results

Write a question-led narrative with a conditional ending. State why the question matters, what prior work establishes, the competing accounts, and the observations that would distinguish them. Do not draft a result-bearing title as though an anticipated gain has occurred.

For a planned comparison, consider plausible outcomes: support for the prediction, an estimate in the opposite direction, a precise small effect where the design permits that conclusion, an inconclusive estimate, and execution or measurement failure. Record the resulting interpretations as possibilities, not results. For each, identify which claims would remain defensible and what would still be unknown. Do not force every design into these branches if its epistemic aims differ.

Record a prespecified contingent analysis in the plan when appropriate; a prose decision tree is not a registry submission. Keep known development observations and held-out confirmation separate. A new protocol cannot erase previous exposure to the same evaluation cases.

## After results

Reconcile the complete original plan before choosing a paper center. Link each planned item to a result or an explicit disposition. Then separate:

1. What was observed under the actual design.
2. What the observation supports under stated assumptions.
3. What alternative account remains possible.
4. What the current evidence cannot decide.

Mark newly generated hypotheses and analyses with their actual timing. An unexpected finding can be central if its origin is clear and the planned evidence is fairly accounted for. It must not inherit confirmatory status from another test.

An expanded paper can connect several studies around one question, but preserve which study generated the hypothesis and which tested it. Check overlap of samples, source corpora, annotations, model selection, and evaluators before calling the studies independent confirmation.

## Choosing among narratives

Use the [branch-and-select workflow](branch-and-select.md): prepare compact trees and reader previews for genuinely different framings, then let the author select before expanding one. A comparison table alone is not enough to review a narrative. Use this matrix alongside the branch cards:

| Candidate framing | Evidence explained | Difficult or conflicting evidence | Extra assumptions | Closest precedent | Decision or next discriminating observation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution A | Source IDs | Source IDs | Specific assumptions | Verified source/version | Supported, narrowed, unresolved, or rejected |

Do not equate rhetorical appeal with evidential support. A descriptive or diagnostic contribution may be defensible when a mechanism or superiority claim is not. If a new analysis would distinguish narratives, describe it as proposed and seek authorization only when execution falls outside the task.

## From selected tree to paper

After the author selects a branch or explicitly delegates the choice, write one sentence per beat before drafting paragraphs:

| Beat | The reader should be able to answer |
|---|---|
| Problem | What decision or phenomenon matters, and to whom? |
| Knowledge and gap | What does the closest prior work establish, and what remains unresolved? |
| Question | What specific uncertainty does this study address? |
| Design | What can the chosen comparison or observation actually identify? |
| Findings | Which evidence changes the answer, including important null or conflicting branches? |
| Interpretation | Why does that evidence warrant the proposed claim over alternatives? |
| Contribution and boundary | What can now be said, for which settings, and what remains open? |

This is a reader dependency order, not a mandated IMRaD section list. Adapt it for qualitative, theoretical, design, and mixed-method papers. A qualitative finding may need a contextual excerpt plus deviant cases; a theoretical claim needs a derivation and assumptions; a system claim needs a matched comparison. Avoid treating all evidence as a numerical hypothesis test.

For every figure, name the inferential job: expose a process, compare conditions, show uncertainty or heterogeneity, establish an ownership link, or display a counterexample. Keep actual chart data, units, captions, and source IDs. Do not invent a visual because the outline has an empty slot.

Before manuscript handoff, distinguish a proposed editorial question/title from the historically stated RQ/hypothesis. Supply the selected framing, alternative interpretations, claim IDs, section destinations, source/figure pointers, planned-item dispositions, and unresolved limitations. A paper editor should not have to infer which parts are results and which parts are proposed.
