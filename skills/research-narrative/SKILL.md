# Research narrative

Help the reader understand what changed in our knowledge and why the evidence warrants that change. Produce a usable narrative tree and paper outline, not a more flattering account of the study.

Keep three linked views: **commitments** (what was planned and when), **argument** (what supports or challenges each claim), and **reader sequence** (the order that makes the argument understandable). Reorder exposition freely within the user's scope; preserve research history and evidence status.

## Start with the actual record

Find the latest user-edited manuscript or outline, research questions, registration/protocol versions, amendments, safe result summaries, source figures, and closest literature. Reuse their identifiers and owning records. A chat summary is a lead; inspect the referenced evidence before relying on it. Do not open sealed evaluation data just to write a narrative.

Record the intended audience and task: plan a future paper, interpret completed work, diagnose a draft, or connect several studies. Infer routine editorial choices. Ask only when an unresolved choice changes the scientific argument. An explicit request for a narrative does not authorize manuscript rewriting, new experiments, registration, or publication.

Check registration status rather than assuming it. Distinguish a verifiable registration, a locally saved prospective plan, a retrospective reconstruction, and an unknown record. Registration timing, prior data exposure, and departures determine what can be described as planned or confirmatory. A preregistered plan may itself contain exploratory work. Keep original wording and timestamps; never create historical H1/H2 labels that appear to come from the original protocol.

For the rationale behind this approach, read [cross-domain methods](references/cross-domain-methods.md). For graph fields and arrow meanings, read [argument map](references/argument-map.md). For the task's research phase, read [planning and interpretation](references/planning-and-interpretation.md).

## Ground motivation and findings in the literature

For a substantive narrative, read [literature grounding](references/literature-grounding.md). Literature is part of the argument at every stage, not a background section added after choosing a story.

Build the motivation from established knowledge, the closest relevant studies, and a current search for recent developments. Explain the substantive uncertainty and why resolving it matters. State what our design can address and what the actual findings contribute. Do not invent an unstudied gap to accommodate a preferred result. A replication, boundary condition, synthesis, or unresolved discrepancy can be a contribution without a wholly untouched topic.

For each central finding, identify the closest published result or theoretical expectation, what resembles our finding, what differs, and whether it corroborates, extends, qualifies, contradicts, or cannot yet be directly compared with that work. Check population, task, constructs, measurements, design, and uncertainty before assigning that relationship. Use the literature to examine rival explanations as well as to support the favored interpretation.

Verify current primary sources and publication versions when developing or refreshing the narrative. Include foundational work when needed; relevance and evidential quality govern inclusion, not recency alone. Record the search date/scope and distinguish peer-reviewed publications, preprints, and inaccessible or partially inspected sources. If current searching is unavailable, deliver a provisional framing with the precise unresolved literature questions rather than claim a current or novel contribution.

Keep two distinct statements: why the question was worth asking, grounded in knowledge available then; and what the findings mean in light of the literature now. Later publications may change positioning without becoming the historical reason for the original hypothesis.

## Build the argument before the prose

1. **Inventory obligations.** Account for every research question, hypothesis, primary outcome, and planned analysis in the selected study. For each, link its wording/version, exposure/timing, analysis/result, deviations, and manuscript destination. Retain null, mixed, contradictory, inconclusive, and unexecuted branches. Use an explicit reason when a planned item is unavailable or out of the paper's scope.
2. **State the uncertainty.** Build a source-linked progression from established and recent research to the unresolved question and its significance. Compare the closest work for both resemblance and difference, including near precedents and counterexamples. Explain why the remaining uncertainty is meaningful rather than just an untried combination. Mark novelty unresolved when access or evidence is insufficient.
3. **Identify consequential claims.** Connect each to an exact result or passage, the reasoning that links evidence to claim, scope conditions, and the strongest rival interpretation. Link each central finding to the published findings or theory it corroborates, extends, qualifies, contradicts, or cannot directly be compared with. Preserve sample/analysis units, denominators, effect sizes and uncertainty where relevant. Evidence derived from the same cases is not independent replication.
4. **Compare plausible framings.** When interpretation is genuinely open, consider two or three substantively different accounts, including a narrower explanation. Compare evidence coverage, assumptions, counterevidence, closest prior work, and what would discriminate between accounts. Avoid arbitrary narrative scores. Do not invent rival stories merely to reach a quota.
5. **Choose a provisional central contribution.** Prefer the account requiring the fewest unsupported inferential steps and fairly representing the study. A method advantage, boundary condition, descriptive account, measurement problem, null finding, or unresolved question can all organize a paper. If several accounts remain observationally indistinguishable, retain that ambiguity rather than select a winner through prose.
6. **Draw a readable tree.** Put the contribution at the root, supporting subclaims on branches, and source-linked evidence at leaves. Include prior-work nodes for essential premises and comparisons, visibly distinct from our own findings. Connect assumptions and counterevidence to the precise inference they affect. Reuse evidence IDs across branches instead of duplicating findings. Use a compact tree for reading and cross-links for shared evidence; the complete argument can be a graph. Arrows express logical roles, not chronology or causal effects unless explicitly modeled.
7. **Create the reader sequence.** Expand a one-sentence takeaway into a short abstract spine, then sections and figures. For each section, name the reader's question, its answer, claim/evidence IDs, and the question it raises next. A figure earns its place by resolving an uncertainty or enabling a comparison. Define necessary concepts before using them. Disclose the answer early rather than manufacturing suspense.

Use [the narrative template](assets/narrative-template.md) as needed; remove empty scaffolding. A small question may need only a short argument and a diagram. A large project may need the full register and graph. Do not create a second experiment database.

## Keep inference honest while improving the story

- Separate an observed result, its interpretation, a causal mechanism, and a future prediction. A causal story needs identification assumptions and evidence; a flowchart cannot supply them.
- A failed significance test is not evidence of equivalence by itself. Preserve interval width and design sensitivity. A counterexample can refute a universal claim without estimating prevalence.
- A model improvement must survive the relevant matched controls. Track differences in data, supervision, compute, information, and downstream rules before attributing gains to a named mechanism.
- Qualitative, descriptive, theoretical, and exploratory papers need an appropriate contribution and evidence structure; do not force them into experimental H1/H2 or universal preregistration requirements. Preserve disconfirming cases and the scope of interpretations.
- Keep expected-result branches separate from observed-result branches. Results may change the final framing without becoming predictions made in advance. Record departures and new questions with their actual timing.
- Do not bury decisive counterevidence in an appendix while presenting an unqualified headline. Concision may remove repetition, not the condition that makes a claim true.

## Deliver and hand off

Return the recommended framing, meaningful alternatives if unresolved, a narrative tree, a section/figure sequence, and the evidence or decisions still needed. Include the literature-to-motivation chain and finding-to-literature comparisons, scaled to the task, plus a traceable commitments register when there is a plan to reconcile. Keep technical bookkeeping out of the audience-facing prose while preserving source pointers in the working artifact.

Before handing off, trace every contribution claim back to evidence and every planned obligation forward to a reported disposition. Check that the motivation's premises and gap have source support and that every central finding is contextualized against relevant prior work. Check that changing or removing a decisive result or closest precedent would appropriately change the conclusion. Check reused evidence, contradictions, unstated warrants, novelty, and exposure status. This is an editorial/source check, not proof that the skill improves scientific outcomes.

Use `paper-factory` when manuscript drafting or revision is requested, `presentation-factory` for a talk, and an available experiment-notebook workflow when authorized experiments or amendments are actually being recorded. Those skills are optional handoff targets; this skill can deliver a narrative on its own. Preserve IDs and claim qualifiers across the handoff. Do not launch experiments to fill a narrative gap without authorization.
